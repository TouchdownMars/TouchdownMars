# Touchdown Mars: First Light · Teacher and Parent Card

**Ages:** 7-12, in two bands: **Cadet** (7-9: 4 power cells, 5 phases, one-sentence cards) and **Commander** (10-12: 6 power cells, 6 phases, two-sentence cards, Mission Control replies one phase late). The game starts in Commander; an adult can switch the age band in the corner group or in Settings before the child plays.
**Time:** one mission is designed for about 5-8 minutes (31 sols on Mars). Mouse left click only. English or Turkish.
**Setting:** one child, or two children deciding together at one computer.

Not endorsed by NASA.

## What children practise

1. **Trade-offs with limited power.** There are never enough power cells for AIR, GREENHOUSE and SHELTER at once. In the game's words: "Power you give to one, the others lose." Every choice costs something.
2. **Delayed communication with Mars.** "Call home" sends a message to Earth. On Mars the reply comes later (one phase later for Commanders) and not at all during the blackout, so the plan has to be made before the advice arrives.
3. **Radiation shelter.** When a solar storm card arrives, a strong SHELTER (or going inside) protects the crew; staying outside for science records a storm exposure.
4. **Plants take time.** GREENHOUSE changes show up only in the next phase. Real lettuce on the space station grew for 33 days and peppers for 137, so only a small lettuce harvest fits in a 31-sol mission.
5. **Real, dated NASA events.** After each choice the card flips to "What really happened?": a real event with its date, instrument, measurement and source (for example the September 2017 solar storm measured by Curiosity, or Apollo 13's air filter fix).

## Discussion questions

**Cadet (7-9)**
1. Which system did you give the most power to? What happened to the others?
2. When the solar storm came, where was the crew safest? Why?
3. Why did Mission Control's message take time to reach you?

**Commander (10-12)**
1. Mission Control's advice was right but late. What did you decide before it arrived, and would you change it?
2. The greenhouse grows one phase after you power it. When is the best time to give it power, and what do you give up?
3. Pick one "What really happened?" card. What did the real crew or rover team have to give up?

For a lost mission, ask the game's own question: "What one thing would you change?" A loss belongs to the plan, never to the child.

## Play the same mission again

- Every mission has a 4-digit **mission seed**, printed on the Mission Report ("Mission seed" and four digits). The same seed gives the same event cards in the same order.
- **Fly again** (after lift-off), **Change one thing and try again** (after a loss) and **Restart same mission** (Settings) replay the same seed on the same computer, so a child can change one decision and compare. From the second game the report shows "Last time: X of 4 stamps. This time: Y of 4."

## Safety and privacy

- No accounts, no sign-up, no names, no chat, no ads, no scores or leaderboards.
- No data is collected. The game saves only local settings on this computer (language, age band, sound, reduce motion, screen mode, last seed and last report).
- Works offline. The only network use is one read of public NASA space-weather data (DONKI) at start for the Today's Sun panel, labelled `LIVE` with the fetch time. Without a connection the panel shows `ARCHIVE` (September 2017) and the game plays the same. The panel never changes gameplay.
- No person's face or voice is used; crew members wear closed visors.

## Why it is built this way

- The rule is shown first, then the child tries it: in grades 2-5, letting children fail first did not help (Sinha & Kapur 2021, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.3102/00346543211019105).
- Younger children hold fewer things in mind at once, so Cadet decides two systems per phase, not three (Cowan 2016, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4800832).
- No tutorial screen; teaching happens in the first moves (Andersen et al. 2012, https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf; UNICEF RITEC design deck, https://www.unicef.org/childrightsandbusiness/media/1146/file).
- The replay loop matches a NASA K-12 STEM inventory item, "When a project does not work out I can think of other ways to do it" (NASA K-12 Outcome Assessment, https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/k-12-oa-evaluation-report-brief-final-508a.pdf).
